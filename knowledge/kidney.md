# Kidney

## Local Dynamics

### Renal Perfusion and Filtration

Blood delivered to the kidney is filtered across the glomerular filtration barrier.

Glomerular filtration depends on renal perfusion, glomerular hydrostatic pressure, renal vascular resistance, and the filtration properties of the glomerulus.

Changes in afferent and efferent arteriolar tone can alter renal blood flow, glomerular pressure, and glomerular filtration rate.

Local autoregulatory mechanisms help stabilize renal blood flow and filtration across a physiological range of perfusion pressures.

Large reductions in renal perfusion can reduce glomerular filtration despite autoregulation.

### Tubular Reabsorption and Secretion

Filtered water and solutes pass through successive nephron segments, where substances can be selectively reabsorbed from tubular fluid or secreted into it.

Urinary excretion depends on the combined effects of glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption, and tubular secretion.

Tubular transport depends on transporter activity, electrochemical gradients, tubular flow, hormonal signaling, and cellular metabolic capacity.

### Sodium and Water Handling

Most filtered sodium is normally reabsorbed along the nephron.

Sodium reabsorption is influenced by tubular sodium delivery, angiotensin II, aldosterone, sympathetic activity, natriuretic peptides, and local tubular transport mechanisms.

Increased sodium reabsorption decreases urinary sodium excretion, whereas reduced sodium reabsorption increases urinary sodium excretion.

Water reabsorption depends on osmotic gradients and segment-specific water permeability.

ADH increases water permeability in the distal nephron and collecting duct, promoting water reabsorption and production of more concentrated urine.

Reduced ADH signaling decreases collecting-duct water permeability and promotes excretion of more dilute urine.

Through these mechanisms, the kidney contributes to regulation of extracellular fluid volume and plasma osmolality.

### Glucose Handling

Glucose is freely filtered at the glomerulus and is normally reabsorbed in the proximal tubule through saturable transport mechanisms.

At low or moderate filtered glucose loads, most filtered glucose is reabsorbed.

When filtered glucose exceeds tubular reabsorptive capacity, glucose remains in tubular fluid and urinary glucose excretion increases.

Glucose retained in tubular fluid increases osmotic load, reduces water reabsorption, and can promote osmotic diuresis.

### Potassium Handling

Potassium undergoes glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption, and regulated tubular secretion.

Distal potassium secretion depends on potassium availability, distal sodium delivery, tubular flow, aldosterone signaling, and electrochemical gradients.

Aldosterone generally increases distal potassium secretion while promoting sodium reabsorption.

Changes in these mechanisms alter urinary potassium excretion.

### Acid–Base Regulation

Filtered bicarbonate is extensively reabsorbed along the nephron.

Tubular cells can secrete hydrogen ions and generate new bicarbonate.

Renal ammonium production and excretion provide an additional mechanism for net acid elimination.

Renal acid and bicarbonate handling changes in response to acid–base conditions, potassium availability, tubular transport, and local metabolic state.

These processes contribute to regulation of extracellular acid–base balance.

### Urea and Waste Handling

Urea and other small metabolic waste products can be filtered and undergo varying degrees of tubular reabsorption or secretion.

Their urinary excretion depends on filtration, tubular handling, and urine flow.

Urea recycling also contributes to maintenance of the renal medullary osmotic gradient.

### Calcium and Phosphate Handling

Filtered calcium and phosphate undergo regulated tubular reabsorption.

Parathyroid hormone and other regulatory signals can modify renal calcium and phosphate handling.

Changes in tubular transport alter urinary calcium and phosphate excretion.

### Renin Secretion

Juxtaglomerular cells in the kidney secrete renin.

Reduced renal perfusion pressure, reduced sodium-chloride delivery to the macula densa, and increased renal sympathetic stimulation can increase renin secretion.

Increased renal perfusion or sodium-chloride delivery can suppress renin secretion.

Renin initiates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and contributes to regulation of sodium balance, extracellular fluid volume, and arterial pressure.

### Other Endocrine and Metabolic Functions

The kidney produces erythropoietin in response to reduced renal oxygen availability.

The kidney converts vitamin D to its active form and thereby contributes to calcium and phosphate regulation.

Renal tubular transport requires substantial ATP production and oxygen consumption, so reduced oxygen availability can impair energy-dependent transport.

The kidney can also perform gluconeogenesis under appropriate metabolic conditions.
