# Small Intestine

## Local Dynamics

### Nutrient Digestion and Absorption

Digested carbohydrates are converted primarily into monosaccharides that can be absorbed across the intestinal epithelium.

Glucose and other monosaccharides are transported from the intestinal lumen into enterocytes and subsequently transferred toward the circulation.

Dietary proteins are digested into amino acids and small peptides, which can be absorbed by intestinal epithelial cells.

Dietary lipids are processed into absorbable lipid products, taken up by enterocytes, reassembled into triglycerides, and packaged into chylomicrons for transport out of the intestine.

### Water and Electrolyte Transport

Water moves across the intestinal epithelium according to osmotic gradients generated by solute transport.

Sodium, chloride, potassium, and other electrolytes undergo regulated absorption and secretion along the intestine.

Changes in nutrient and electrolyte transport therefore influence intestinal water absorption or secretion.

### Incretin Secretion

Specialized enteroendocrine cells sense nutrients within the intestinal lumen.

Oral glucose and other nutrients stimulate secretion of incretin hormones, particularly glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide and glucagon-like peptide-1.

Incretin secretion generally increases following nutrient ingestion and provides an endocrine signal associated with the fed state.

### Gastrointestinal Hormone Secretion

Enteroendocrine cells release multiple hormones in response to nutrients, luminal contents, and neural signals.

These hormones can regulate intestinal motility, secretion, digestion, satiety-related signaling, and coordination of nutrient handling.

### Motility

Smooth-muscle activity moves and mixes intestinal contents.

Enteric neural circuits, autonomic input, local distension, and gastrointestinal hormones regulate intestinal motility.

Changes in motility alter the rate at which luminal contents are mixed, transported, and presented for absorption.

### Perfusion and Oxygen Dependence

Intestinal perfusion supports epithelial transport, nutrient processing, and transfer of absorbed substances away from the intestinal wall.

Reduced perfusion or oxygen availability can impair active transport and intestinal metabolic function.
