# Liver

## State

The liver is a major organ for regulating carbohydrate storage and the exchange of glucose with the circulation.

Local states relevant to glucose regulation include:

* net hepatic glucose uptake
* net hepatic glucose release
* hepatic glycogen store
* glycogenesis
* glycogenolysis
* gluconeogenesis
* hepatic glucose utilization
* insulin receptor activity
* glucagon receptor activity
* GLUT-mediated glucose transport, especially bidirectional hepatic glucose transport
* glucokinase activity
* glycogen synthase activity
* glycogen phosphorylase activity
* adenylate cyclase activity
* cyclic AMP signaling
* protein kinase activity involved in glucagon signaling

The liver can both remove glucose from blood and return glucose to blood. Its glycogen store is therefore a dynamically accessible whole-body glucose reservoir.

## Inputs

### Circulating glucose

Glucose reaches hepatocytes from the blood.

Hepatic glucose transport is bidirectional and is not itself dependent on insulin. Consequently, the direction of net glucose movement depends on extracellular glucose concentration and intracellular glucose metabolism.

Once glucose enters hepatocytes, phosphorylation by glucokinase helps retain glucose inside the cell and makes it available for glycogen synthesis and other metabolic pathways.

### Insulin

Circulating insulin binds insulin receptors on hepatocytes.

Insulin signaling informs the liver that circulating nutrient availability is high.

### Glucagon

Circulating glucagon binds glucagon receptors on hepatocytes.

Glucagon receptor activation stimulates intracellular cyclic AMP signaling and promotes pathways that mobilize hepatic glucose.

### Epinephrine

Adrenergic stimulation can activate hepatic glycogenolysis and contribute to glucose release during acute physiological stress.

## Dynamics

When insulin increases during a glucose-rich state, hepatic metabolism shifts toward glucose uptake and storage.

Insulin suppresses glycogen breakdown by reducing glycogen phosphorylase activity and promotes glycogen formation by increasing glycogen synthase activity.

Insulin also promotes hepatic handling of incoming glucose by increasing glucokinase-dependent phosphorylation and favors conversion of glucose into glycogen and other stored forms.

At the same time, insulin suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis. The combined result is a reduction in net hepatic glucose output.

When glucagon increases, glucagon receptor signaling activates adenylate cyclase, raises cyclic AMP, and activates a protein-kinase-dependent phosphorylation cascade.

This activates glycogen phosphorylase and promotes glycogenolysis, rapidly mobilizing the hepatic glycogen store.

Glucagon also promotes gluconeogenesis, providing a slower mechanism for continued hepatic glucose production when external glucose availability is low.

When blood glucose is elevated and insulin is present, glucose entry, phosphorylation, and glycogen synthesis favor net hepatic glucose uptake.

During fasting or low-insulin/high-glucagon conditions, glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis favor net hepatic glucose release.

The liver therefore changes the direction and magnitude of its glucose flux according to the combination of circulating glucose, insulin, and glucagon rather than according to any single signal alone.

## Outputs

The externally observable glucose-related output of the liver is its net glucose flux to the circulation.

This can appear as:

* hepatic glucose release into blood
* hepatic glucose uptake from blood

Changes in glycogen storage, glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, receptor activity, and intracellular signaling remain local states unless they alter this net flux.
