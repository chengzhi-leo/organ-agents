# Liver

## Local Dynamics

### Glucose and Glycogen Metabolism

The liver can take up circulating glucose, use it for energy, store it as glycogen, or release glucose into the circulation.

Glucose entering hepatocytes can be phosphorylated by glucokinase and directed toward glycolysis, glycogen synthesis, or other metabolic pathways.

Hepatic glycogen provides a rapidly accessible carbohydrate reserve. Increased glycogen synthesis increases hepatic glycogen stores, whereas glycogenolysis mobilizes stored glycogen and can contribute to hepatic glucose release.

Net hepatic glucose uptake or release depends on glucose availability, glycogen stores, hormonal signaling, and local metabolic state.

### Hormonal Regulation

Increased circulating insulin increases net hepatic glucose uptake, promotes glycogen synthesis and storage of available metabolic substrates, and suppresses glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.

Glucagon promotes hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis and suppresses glycogen synthesis.

Catecholamines, particularly epinephrine, can rapidly stimulate hepatic glycogenolysis.

The balance among insulin, glucagon, catecholamine signaling, substrate availability, and hepatic energy state determines the direction of hepatic carbohydrate metabolism.

### Gluconeogenesis

The liver can synthesize glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors including lactate, glycerol, and glucogenic amino acids.

Gluconeogenesis increases when suitable precursors and metabolic energy are available and is promoted by glucagon and catecholamine signaling while being suppressed by insulin.

Lactate taken up by the liver can be converted into glucose or used in other metabolic pathways.

### Lipid and Ketone Metabolism

Circulating fatty acids can be taken up by hepatocytes and either oxidized for energy or incorporated into triglycerides and other lipids.

When carbohydrate and energy availability are high, the liver can synthesize fatty acids and triglycerides and export triglycerides in very-low-density lipoproteins.

When fatty-acid availability is high and insulin signaling is relatively low, fatty-acid oxidation and ketogenesis increase.

Ketone bodies produced by the liver can be released into the circulation.

### Amino-Acid and Nitrogen Metabolism

Circulating amino acids can be used for protein synthesis, converted into metabolic intermediates, or catabolized.

Glucogenic amino acids can provide substrates for gluconeogenesis.

Nitrogen generated during amino-acid metabolism, including ammonia, can be converted into urea through the hepatic urea cycle.

### Bile and Bilirubin Handling

The liver synthesizes bile acids from cholesterol and secretes bile.

Bilirubin can be taken up by hepatocytes, conjugated, and secreted into bile.

These processes contribute to lipid digestion and provide routes for elimination of bilirubin, cholesterol, and other substances.

### Protein Synthesis and Detoxification

The liver synthesizes and secretes major plasma proteins, including albumin, coagulation proteins, and transport proteins.

Hepatocytes also metabolize many drugs, hormones, toxins, and other xenobiotic compounds through enzymatic modification and conjugation.

### Perfusion and Oxygen Dependence

Hepatic perfusion determines delivery of oxygen, hormones, and metabolic substrates to hepatocytes.

Reduced perfusion or oxygen availability can limit oxidative metabolism and other energy-dependent hepatic processes.
